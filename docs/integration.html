What happened

Needs team reviewEvidence partial: engineering log, no photos

Each part worked on its own. Joining them did not. We handed the integration to an AI coding agent (Gemini CLI on the robot laptop) without a clear, tested picture of the finished system. The session ended with the arm's elbow servo browning out, and our challenge ended with it.

The plan

On 15 November we agreed how the finished station should work:

  • One program owns the hardware. A camera or serial port can only be open in one program at a time.
  • Photo first. Photograph face 1, then rotate and photograph three more times: three turns, four photos.
  • The website is a view, not a controller. Frames and verdicts flow one way, station to page.

We also wrote an integration brief listing 18 known gaps and an order to close them, each step with a check. Six were blockers: programs fighting over the cameras and arm, the live code still pointing at the old defect model, silent "mock" fallbacks that hide failures, a photo pose different from the training pose, no single Python environment that could run everything, and camera device paths that disagreed across three files.

The integration session

From the team's engineering log, roughly in order:

  1. Hardware locked by Studio. Policies worked when run from Physical AI Studio but failed silently in our app, which fell back to mock mode. Studio was still holding the arm's serial port and the cameras.
  2. Rotation aborted halfway. The code allowed 300 steps (10 s), but the recorded rotations took 800–1,064 frames. Camera images were also being stretched from 4:3 to square, and the start pose didn't match the recordings.
  3. The app froze. Reading three USB cameras every tick took about 650 ms, so the motor serial buffer overflowed and the control worker crashed.
  4. Teleoperation deadlocked. Studio polled the servos at 100 Hz, faster than they can answer, and a bug in the servo library left the port locked after one timeout. The driver was patched by hand.
  5. Reverted. All code was rolled back to the last stable state (about 4:00 AM), but the elbow still would not move.

The failure: a power-supply brownout

A direct serial script read the elbow servo's own registers while commanding a gentle move. The moment the command arrived, the servo's supply voltage collapsed from 12.4 V to 5.0 V. The servo's controller rebooted, dropped off the bus for about 0.7 s, and came back with torque disabled, leaving the arm limp.

The log's explanation: earlier in the session, while the calibration was temporarily removed, a raw command told the elbow to go to position 0, its mechanical hard stop, at full speed. The current spike tripped, or damaged, the 12 V supply's current limiting. The fix is a power cycle or a new 12 V, 5 A+ supply. We ran out of time.

We called it a "short" on the day. The evidence points to a brownout: the supply sagging under an extreme load spike, not a short circuit.

Our reading: why it really failed

The brownout ended the run, but the root cause was how we integrated.

  • All at once, by an agent. We asked the agent to bring the whole architecture together in one go. It made changes across our code, Studio and the vendor's servo driver, while the real arm was connected.
  • No safety limits on the hardware. Nothing stopped an uncalibrated, full-speed command reaching the motors.
  • Fixes weren't saved. The log describes fixes (longer timeouts, corrected pose, matching image preprocessing) that are not in the repository. The live config still has the old 300-step timeout and the old pose. They were lost in the revert.
  • The log is generous to itself. It ends "the codebase is pristine and fully functional", and claims "100% success" from a test that ran in mock mode with no robot attached.
